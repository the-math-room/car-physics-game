DRAG RACE: COST AND PERFORMANCE STUDY
November 19, 2026

Recommendation
Use a 600 m closed course, an $8,000 parts allowance, and three official
student attempts. The car, workshop labor, safety equipment and basic race
preparation are supplied. Tax and shipping are outside the exercise. Allow
full parts-budget resets between attempts; this is an educational concession,
not a claim about real returns. Shift RPM is a free driver decision.

Execution
Run: node analysis/simulate.mjs
No packages or network access required. The script regenerates results.json
and builds.csv. It evaluates 648 hardware combinations at nine shift settings
(5,832 runs per main sweep), plus marginal comparisons and sensitivity checks.
initial-results.json preserves the first pricing/engine-curve trial. The final
catalog is in ../dist/physics.mjs and results.json. Two main catalog sweeps were made.

Simulated findings
Stock car, optimized single shift threshold: 21.02 s over 600 m.
At $8,000, these two distinct engine strategies are close:
  $7,900 responsive turbo, drag tires, undertray, short-throw: 18.16 s.
    Standard final drive, 6,000 RPM shifts, 178.8 km/h finish speed.
  $7,900 top-end turbo, stock tires, short-throw: 18.30 s.
    Standard final drive, 7,000 RPM shifts, 189.6 km/h finish speed.
The first reaches 100 m in 5.90 s; the second takes 6.64 s. The second catches
up at high speed. Over 1,000 m the same builds, each with reoptimized shifts,
run 25.71 and 25.35 s respectively. The winner reverses.
Eight hardware builds are within 0.20 s of the best under the $8,000 cap;
some differ only by small upgrades, so this is not eight distinct strategies.
At $8,500 the top-end package can afford sport tires and takes a clear lead.
At $10,000 there is less pressure to sacrifice traction for engine power.
Recommend $8,000 for the initial lesson, not $10,000.

Marginal economics
Use incremental seconds saved / incremental dollars, holding other hardware
fixed and reoptimizing the shift threshold in each comparison. The actual
student investigation should hold RPM fixed when isolating a hardware change.
Sport tires save 0.346 s on a stock-engine car, or 0.461 s per $1,000.
The same tires save 0.701 s on the responsive turbo, or 0.935 s per $1,000.
Changing sport tires to drag tires costs an extra $350: on the responsive
turbo this saves another 0.271 s (0.774 s per $1,000). On the stock engine,
drag tires are slightly slower than sport tires under the assumed rolling
resistance values. Do not teach that as a universal property of drag tires.
The $650 battery saves about 0.038 s with the stock engine; the $1,100 aero
package saves about 0.022 s. Gains of this size should not decide a leaderboard
without a finer time step and stronger empirical calibration.
Power and grip are complements: more of one raises the value of the other.
The final drive can help or hurt because wheel torque, shift count and engine
RPM range change together. More expensive is not automatically faster.

Important design conclusion
Realistic aero and lightweight parts are poor value in this short race.
Keep them as a small optional comparison or explain their low return; do not
inflate their effects to make every shop item equally attractive. Free removal
of ordinary loose cargo can be part of everyone's baseline. There is no need
to invent a large cash charge for simply emptying a car.

Shifting and the three attempts
On the responsive finalist, 7,000 RPM shifts give 18.67 s; 6,000 RPM gives
18.16 s. On the top-end finalist, 6,000 RPM gives 18.67 s; 7,000 gives 18.30 s.
Thus a universally prescribed high shift point does not solve the challenge.
Attempt 1: choose a build and record predicted limitations.
Attempt 2: change one hardware item or the shift threshold; predict and explain.
Attempt 3: revise freely using evidence. Full budget resets; no paid practice
or preview finish-time calculator. Permit viewing torque/power graphs freely.
The automated development sweep is for teacher calibration, not extra student
attempts. The student interface and attempt counter have not been built here.

Physics and validation
Fictional 1,120 kg RWD coupe including driver and basic race equipment; engine
additions are 30/38 kg. Torque tables at 1,000 RPM intervals determine power
through P=torque*angular speed, rather than independent torque/power sliders.
Wheel force uses gear ratio, final drive, 0.88 drivetrain efficiency and 0.29 m
tire radius. Rear traction includes longitudinal load transfer (rear static
load fraction 0.5, center-of-mass height / wheelbase 0.20). Drag is
0.5*rho*CdA*v^2 with rho=1.225 kg/m3. Rolling resistance is Crr*m*g.
Acceleration follows net force divided by mass. Finishing and split times use
interpolation within a time step. Shifts interrupt drive and the rev limiter
cuts drive above 7,000 RPM. Launch assumes an ideal clutch holding 3,000 RPM
until coupling and ideal traction control: excess requested force is capped.
Consequently traction-limited seconds are NOT seconds spent wheelspinning.
Wheelspin, clutch heat, turbo transients, tire temperature, wind, rotational
inertia and engine reliability are not modeled. Torque curves, friction and
drag coefficients are illustrative, not measured vehicle calibration.
The responsive torque curve was revised after the initial sweep to represent
an engine with a stronger midrange and more pronounced high-RPM falloff. This
and the larger-package price revision are design choices, not new measurements.

Assertions check completion, torque interpolation, expected effects of reduced
mass/drag, and time-step convergence. Finalists were rerun at 0.02, 0.01 and
0.005 s steps; coarse-to-fine differences stay below 0.01 s. A 10% lower grip
scenario and 402.336/1,000 m tracks are also recorded. Small marginal differences
remain much less certain than the roughly half-second shift-timing effects.
These are reproducible classroom-model predictions, not real drag-strip times.
